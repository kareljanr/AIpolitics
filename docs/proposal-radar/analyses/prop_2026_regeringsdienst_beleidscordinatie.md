## Executive judgment (fair)

Belgium’s federal centre **does** have a real coordination problem: multi-party coalitions, bilingual dual structures, weak horizontal priority-setting, and FODs that can ignore political clocks. A steelman for a small **delivery unit** next to the PM is not absurd (UK-style Implementation Unit literature exists).

What is absurd is **selling bureaucratic expansion as reform** while the same coalition markets spending discipline — and doing it by:

1. Creating a **new named service** rather than fixing existing Kanselarij / BOSA / SPM tools.  
2. Staffing it with **cabinet-profile people** (loyalty and speed) instead of strengthening neutral permanent capacity.  
3. Accepting **dual FODs** (two parallel federal public services for related tasks) — the opposite of simplification.  
4. Publishing **no staff/euro card** while asking taxpayers to trust “it will pay for itself in better decisions.”

This scores as **Dom / bad**: classic Belgian **administratieve lasagne**. Reject unless redesigned as a time-boxed delivery unit that **closes** other layers and publishes FTE/€.

## Steelman (extensive, good faith)

### 1. Coalition centres fail at horizontal priorities

- Five-party Arizona must align budgets, energy, migration, defence, EU positions.  
- Line ministries optimise local portfolios; PM needs a **force-multiplier** for cross-cutting files.  
- Existing FODs can be slow, process-heavy, and risk-averse — not always wrong, but costly for crisis windows.

### 2. “Cabinet people” are not only patronage

- Steelman: people who understand political trade-offs and can draft decisions that survive kern/MR meetings.  
- Pure classic civil service sometimes optimises for procedure over outcome. A hybrid “political-administrative” cell can unblock.

### 3. Better coordination can save money *if* it kills bad spend

- One blocked prestige project or one faster fiscal package can theoretically outweigh a dozen salaries.  
- Steelman ROI: prevention of Smaakhaven-class errors and faster DOGE-style cuts.

### 4. International analogues exist

- Centre-of-government reform, delivery units, policy hubs — not uniquely Belgian folly.  
- If temporary, KPI-bound, and **substituting** for cabinet bloat, it can be modern statecraft.

### 5. Dual FODs might be path-dependent politics

- Steelman: bilingual/community realities force dual structures; a coordination service is a bandage, not the disease.  
- (Weak steelman — dual FODs are still a choice to score harshly.)

## 2. Mechanism

Claimed mechanism: **add a central node** with political-operational staff → better aligned decisions → better policy.

Actual risks:

| Design choice | Likely effect |
|---------------|---------------|
| New permanent-ish service | Fixed overhead + mission creep |
| Cabinet-profile staffing | Faster politics; weaker neutral challenge function |
| Dual FODs remain | **More** interfaces, not fewer |
| No published KPIs/FTE | Unaccountable growth |

## 9. Recommendation

**`reject`** as currently described.

If forced to amend into something supportable:

1. **Sunset** 24 months; auto-dissolve unless parliament renews with evaluation.  
2. **Net-zero FTE:** each post filled only by **closing** a cabinet or FOD coordination post (named).  
3. Publish **org chart, grades, annual wage bill**, decision KPIs (cycle time, cross-FOD files closed).  
4. Explicit mission: **kill dual structures**, not staff them.  
5. Permanent analytical capacity stays in **one** strengthened centre (Kanselarij/BOSA), not a parallel brand.

Default abundance path: fewer layers, not a better-named layer.

---

## 10. Falsifier

> We reverse toward **amend/support** if within 18 months official data show (a) **net reduction** in total centre-of-government + cabinet FTEs, (b) measurable cut in cross-ministerial decision lag on a published basket of files, and (c) at least one dual-FOD merge completed with the service as catalyst — all audited.
