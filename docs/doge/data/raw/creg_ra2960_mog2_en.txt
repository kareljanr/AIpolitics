PREAMBLE 
Brussels, 24 January 2025 
Dear reader, 
This document is the result of intensive work by a team of CREG staff and consultants who spent almost 
three months investigating the causes of an unprecedented budget increase in electricity grid 
infrastructure. This increase was brought to light by the CREG, which was subsequently tasked by both 
the executive and the legislative authorities with producing a detailed report on the various figures 
presented by Elia, in particular on the contracts for the 'direct current part' of the energy island by the 
end of January 2025. 
By the time we finish this report, we hope that a pragmatic solution can be found regarding 
confidentiality for the hearing of the CREG on this case, planned for 4 February in the Energy, 
Environment and Climate Committee of the Chamber. Given the social and democratic importance of 
the case, the CREG has redoubled its efforts, so that its analysis is didactic and understandable for 
everyone involved in this a priori complex case (policymakers, members of parliament, industrial grid 
users, experts in the sector but also residential consumers and therefore Belgian citizens).  
This report presents a wealth of information on the various processes that gradually led to the rise and 
eventual derailment of costs. These elements allow us to learn from the past and, based on the various 
recommendations in our conclusion, ensure that the regulator’s control and therefore its effectiveness 
are bolstered, to avoid history repeating itself. 
I wish you pleasant reading,

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY 
Report (RA)2960 on the MOG II project’s budget increases is the result of a methodical and objective 
investigation by the CREG to answer the questions raised by the Minister for Energy and the Chamber 
of Representatives on the Modular Offshore Grid II (MOG II), in line with the parliamentary hearings 
on the matter, on 12 and 27 November 2024. 
The MOG II project has two objectives. The first objective is to integrate wind energy production from 
the Princess Elisabeth Zone (PEZ) into the Belgian and European electricity grid. The development of 
this zone must enable installing 3.15 to 3.5 GW of wind capacity. The second objective of the MOG II 
is to facilitate access to renewable energy produced elsewhere in the North Sea. This is possible thanks 
to interconnections such as Nautilus, which links Belgium with the UK.  
To fulfil these roles, the design for the MOG II, which was approved by the decision-making federal 
authorities, consists of the construction of an artificial island for offshore electricity transmission 
infrastructure. The island needs to be connected to the coast by 2,100 MW of alternating current (AC) 
high-voltage cables, and 1,400 MW of direct current (DC) cables. The main reason for using DC 
technology is the necessity to host offshore interconnections which need DC technology because of 
the long distances to be covered. 
Elia divided the MOG II project into five packages that were contracted separately: 
- 
the artificial island includes the civil engineering works for the caisson-based construction 
for the offshore transmission infrastructure; 
- 
the AC substations include AC transmission infrastructure. The main elements are 220 kV 
substations, 66 kV cabins, 220 kV / 66 kV transformers and shunt reactors to compensate 
for the reactive power. The offshore electrical infrastructure will be installed in steel 
modules that will be built onshore and then transported to the island and installed on the 
island, with the exception of transformers and shunts that will be installed directly on the 
island; 
- 
the AC cables consist of six 220 kV AC cables that connect the island to the coast, and run 
approximately 55 km into the sea;  
- 
the DC substations include an offshore AC/DC converter and an onshore AC/DC converter, 
with a voltage of 525 kV. As is the case for the AC infrastructure, the offshore converter 
will be built in a module; 
- 
The 525-kV DC cable system that connects the offshore AC/DC converter to the onshore 
AC/DC converter.  
The CREG has revealed the significant budget increase for the MOG II project over just three years: 
according to the latest estimates provided by Elia, this budget has increased from € 2.2 billion in 2021 
to € [CONFIDENTIAL: between € 7 and 8] billion at present. Significant cost and/or budget increases 
can be identified for each of the five packages of the MOG II project. The largest cost increases between 
the budget from 2021 and the current budget relate to the AC substations (+ 515%), the DC substations 
(+ [CONFIDENTIAL] %) and the DC cables (+ [CONFIDENTIAL* %).

If the current design and budget of the MOG II remain as is, and without taking into account the costs 
of the offshore wind farms and offshore interconnections that need to be connected to the MOG II, 
the costs for the MOG II that need to be covered by transmission tariffs would amount to around €800 
million per year from 2034 onwards, when the complete electricity infrastructure is commissioned. 
These costs would correspond to €9.8/MWh (or about €34.4/year) for a residential consumer and 
€4.45/MWh for a large industrial consumer directly connected to the Elia transmission system. After 
being commissioned, the depreciation and financing costs - which currently represent more than 85% 
of the costs of the MOG II - will gradually decrease over the next six decades in accordance with the 
depreciation rate: since the electricity infrastructure of the MOG II will be depreciated over a period 
of up to 33 years and only the artificial island over a period of 60 years, these costs will decrease faster 
in the first three decades than in the subsequent three decades. 
Over the past three months, the CREG has been investigating the reasons behind this sharp budgetary 
increase in electricity grid infrastructure. The increase was brought to light by the CREG, who was 
subsequently tasked by both the executive and the legislative authorities to draft a detailed report by 
the end of January 2025 on Elia's various figures, in particular on the DC system of the energy island 
that Elia had not yet contracted.  
Consequently, the CREG focused on the DC system of the electricity infrastructure of the energy island 
in drafting its report. However, the CREG was also able to analyse the AC part of the energy island’s 
electricity infrastructure. Therefore, the conclusions and recommendations in its report go beyond the 
contracts awarded for the DC system of the electricity infrastructure of the energy island. 
The CREG's analyses show that various factors are behind the significant budget increase for the MOG 
II project: 
- 
the transmission system operator (TSO)’s wish to develop a first-of-a-kind infrastructure, 
supported by a government decision in the context of the EU's Recovery Plan, the subsidy 
for which is currently uncertain and limited to €100 million;  
- 
Elia's focus on designing a grid with high availability and operational flexibility, which led 
to assumptions and design choices that increased the cost base compared to typical 
offshore transmission designs. These choices included a single AC node design1 (while Elia 
plans to operate the MOG II in split AC node), a DC connection with a higher capacity 
(2,000 MW instead of 1,400 MW), a more expensive bid from the preferred bidder and a 
high level of redundancy compared to other offshore connection projects currently under 
development elsewhere in the world. Due to these various technical choices made 
unilaterally by Elia after 2021, without informing the authorities or taking into account 
their impact on the electricity consumers’ bills, more equipment had to be ordered than 
originally foreseen in 2021 (and not strictly necessary) to meet the MOG II objectives. In 
addition to costs that Elia had not originally budgeted for in 2021, these orders for 
additional equipment also further increased the MOG II budget due to market 
developments for DC and AC infrastructure over the period 2022-2024; 
 
1 'Single node' refers to the electrical connection between the offshore wind turbines, the offshore HVDC converter and the 
AC export cable.  
The electricity infrastructure that makes the connection between the AC and DC parts, and therefore the single node 
operation, possible was contracted by Elia and will thus be installed on the island. However, initially, the system will be 
operated in split node mode (i.e. without using the connection between the AC and DC components of MOG II), mainly due 
to technical and market integration issues that have not yet been resolved. The single node configuration involves significant 
additional costs and is unusual for offshore connections. If the problems associated with single node operation are not 
resolved, it is highly likely that the installed electrical equipment will never be used. These elements are described in detail 
in chapter 5 of the CREG report.

- 
the market developments for DC and AC infrastructure over the period 2022-2024, due to 
global scarcity in the supply chain, in particular for offshore applications. These market 
developments have resulted in limited offers, with fewer and more expensive bids, less 
flexibility and more risk for Elia;  
- 
keeping to strict timing, whereby unverified assumptions have resulted in premature 
design choices. The main factors in this regard are: the technical and regulatory feasibility 
of using a single AC node and the assumption that the offshore DC and AC substations 
could be built on the island using onshore techniques ('stick-built method2'). In this 
context, it should be emphasised that if, when the design for the MOG II was decided on 
in 2021, it had been clear that the stick-built design of the electricity infrastructure on the 
island was impossible, this would probably have tipped the balance towards the 'with 
platforms' variant, instead of the chosen 'island' variant.  
Based on the detailed analysis carried out by the CREG and an external technical/economic consultant, 
at least €1.570 billion of the identified cost increase can be directly attributed to unilateral (technical) 
choices made by Elia3,4. In addition to the above-mentioned quantified elements, a number of possible 
additional budget increases have been identified that could further (significantly) increase the above-
stated amount. 
The project under review was recently significantly modified, in particular with regard to the 
alternatives proposed by the TSO in December 2024.  
Based on the alternative options and other information provided by Elia on 13 December 2024 and 
8 January 2025, option 3 (Nautilus in point-to-point and 2.1 GW of offshore wind energy in PEZ) seems 
a priori - subject to the working assumptions used in the modelling and subsequent analyses - more 
favourable from the perspective of Belgian consumers. While this option could significantly reduce the 
investment costs, it must also be seen in light of the uncertainties currently surrounding the Nautilus 
project as a whole. Moreover, any possible decision that gives preference to alternative options 
without a DC system on the island would also mean re-examining the optimal size of the island 
(currently 6 hectares) and the design of the MOG II’s electricity system, which could lead to additional 
savings for Belgian consumers. 
At European level, a general rising trend in investment costs can also be seen in the context of large 
infrastructure projects, including offshore grid assets. While tensions in the supply chain, higher 
commodity prices and inflation are the main reasons for the general rise in investment costs, the fact 
remains that such rises can also be exacerbated by initial underestimates of investment costs by the 
TSOs in question. To minimise the impact of this rise, or even escalation, of the investment costs, 
mitigation measures are being considered and discussed by the different countries examined. 
Based on the various conclusions in its detailed report and in order to avoid similar budget increases 
in the future, the CREG has put forward a series of recommendations.

At Belgian level, despite its multidisciplinary expertise, the CREG currently has too limited statutory 
powers in the context of drafting, approving and possible revising the federal development plan of the 
transmission system and in the context of the follow-up of infrastructure projects developed by the 
TSO.  
The genesis of the MOG II project, which the CREG describes in its report on the MOG II project’s 
budget increases, illustrates this point. 
The CREG was confronted with numerous difficulties in the context of monitoring the evolution of 
costs related to the MOG II project. These difficulties included:  
- 
the TSO's lack of transparency regarding all the information which is useful for the proper 
exercise of this monitoring;  
- 
the lack of quantitative and qualitative analyses that are essential before the federal 
government approved the MOG II design in 2021; 
- 
the TSO's refusal to analyse alternative options for the MOG II design at the request of 
the CREG in June 2024, to which no positive follow-up was given until December 2024, in 
order to assess the alternatives and make informed investment choices in the public 
interest and in the interest of Belgian consumers; 
- 
the fact that until October 2024, and despite the explicit request of Elia's board of 
directors in March 2024, the TSO did not proactively and timely inform the competent 
authorities of the significant developments in the project. These include the identified 
budget overrun and the TSO's unilateral decisions, in particular on significant changes 
regarding the choice of technical options that led to the budget increases. The CREG 
would however like to highlight that the cooperation with the TSO proved constructive in 
the context of the drafting of this CREG report on the MOG II project’s budget increases 
after October 2024, and it recommends remaining open and transparent towards the 
CREG. 
The recommendations below aim to improve the regulatory framework for investment projects in grid 
infrastructure. They pertain both to the MOG II project examined here and to other ongoing or future 
investment projects. 
As they address issues of public interest, it would appear to be crucial first and foremost that grid 
infrastructure projects are subject to legal procedures that guarantee, among other things, the 
objectivity and transparency of all qualitative and quantitative analyses underpinning them. This 
requirement is essential to ensure that the results of these analyses only serve the public interest, 
without being influenced by possible conflicts of interest or other specific considerations.

To ensure that the public interest remains the absolute priority in guiding the decisive investment 
choices for Belgium’s energy future, the Electricity Act should also be amended, with regard to:  
- 
the current procedure for preparing, approving and following-up grid development plans, 
in particular by:  
• 
strengthening the role of the public authorities, including the CREG; 
• 
obliging the TSO to present, in the very first phases of elaborating the development 
plan, all possible investment options, supported by quantified analyses to meet the 
same grid needs, so that the authorities have a certain decision-making flexibility. 
Next, the TSO should adequately describe the conceptual design in the 
development plan. Finally, the TSO should avoid putting the competent and 
decision-making authorities in a bind, where alternative choices and possibilities 
are limited or non-existent, to the detriment of the public interest; 
• 
a strict budgetary monitoring mechanism for the TSO’s investment projects, 
including periodic reports to the competent and decision-making authorities. This 
would describe the evolution of costs compared to the initial estimates, as well as 
the explanatory factors behind any identified discrepancies, in order to detect any 
budget overruns or even budget slippages and consider corrective measures in 
good time; 
- 
the tariff framework, to ensure better control of the investment costs and promote 
proactive management by the TSO in the interest of grid users. This could be through a 
new provision in the tariff guidelines to offer a real incentive for strict budgetary 
monitoring; 
- 
the restrictions relating to professional secrecy currently imposed on the CREG, to allow 
genuine parliamentary debate and democratic follow-up on issues of public interest and 
services provided by the CREG, in particular at the request of the public authorities. 
Providing a detailed report is indeed crucial to ensure the efficient functioning of the 
regulator and the parliamentary debate on the matter. 
In addition to these considerations, there is also a need to take into account the possible views of 
different stakeholders as much as possible throughout the process, based on public consultations, so 
as to consolidate public support for decisive investment projects in the context of energy transition.

INTRODUCTION 
This report is the result of a systematic and objective investigation by the CREG to answer the questions 
raised by both the executive (§ 5, chapter 2 Antecedents) and the legislative authorities (§ 10, chapter 
2 Antecedents) regarding the MOG II.  
The CREG solicited DNV for this investigation, a technical-economic consultant selected for its 
advanced expertise, especially in the field of HVDC systems.  
The report is divided into nine chapters, including: 
- 
To provide context: 
• 
an explanation of the existing legal situation (chapter 1 Legal framework: powers 
of the CREG)  
• 
an objective analysis of the background to the case (chapter 3 Background to the 
MOG II project), with justification documents, the process of the various decisions 
taken which are not based on the CREG’s expertise, or only to a limited extent; 
• 
the passing on of the cost of the MOG II in the bills of electricity consumers 
(chapter 4 Estimate of the MOG II impact on electricity bills); 
- 
To provide detailed analysis: 
• 
the technical-economic analysis (chapter 5 Detailed analysis of the investment for 
MOG II); 
• 
the comparative cost-benefit analysis (chapter 6 Comparison of costs and benefits 
of the possible variants for MOG II); 
• 
feedback from European regulators (chapter 7 European regulators). 
- 
Finally, based on the various findings, the report offers recommendations for amending 
the legal framework (chapter 8) to strengthen the role of the regulator. Furthermore, the 
report summarises the different assessments into a general conclusion (chapter 9) to 
prevent similar situations to the MOG II case from recurring in the future. 
Various external analyses and position papers were also considered as a contribution to the discussion 
and as an additional source of inspiration. In particular, in line with its strategic plan 2024-2029 and its 
memorandum 2024, the CREG emphasises again the urgent need to be permanently conscious of 
keeping system costs at an affordable level, in the general interest and in the interest of Belgian 
consumers. In this context, and to paraphrase Febeliec:  
"We don’t know whether an efficient energy transition will be affordable. We do know that 
an inefficient transition will not be affordable. Focus on efficiency. Go for the lowest total 
system cost.” 
In this context, the CREG has a key role to play, taking into account the legal powers entrusted to it.

1. 
LEGAL FRAMEWORK: POWERS OF THE CREG 
1. 
The MOG II is included in the 2024-2034 federal development plan of the electricity transmission 
network, approved by ministerial decree of 5 May 2023. In this context, based on the law of 29 April 
1999 on the organisation of the electricity market (Electricity Act) and the Royal Decree of 12 May 
2024 laying down the procedure for the preparation, approval and publication of the grid development 
plan5, the CREG has very limited powers of action, both a priori, when the federal development plan is 
drawn up, and a posteriori, to amend it after approval by the minister.  
Prior to the approval of the federal development plan by ministerial decree, the CREG's power is 
limited to issuing a simple, non-binding opinion (Articles 6/4, § 2 and 13, § 1 of the Electricity Act). 
Moreover, if the CREG does not respond to the request for an opinion within 40 days, its opinion is 
deemed favourable6. This situation, about which the CREG has repeatedly warned the government7, 
including in its memoranda8, highlights the lack of effective powers and mechanisms to have an 
influence before the minister's approval of the federal development plan.  
Besides the inadequate or even non-existent quantitative and qualitative data on the various studies 
carried out by the transmission system operator (TSO) in this context, on the methodology of the cost-
benefit analyses and on the hypotheses for the selected projects, this time period as provided for by 
the Royal Decree of 12 May 2024 is also largely inadequate to allow the CREG to validly exercise its 
powers. 
After the federal development plan is approved by the minister, the CREG's powers are significantly 
limited. For example, the CREG has no binding prerogative to impose a revision of the plan. In fact, 
article 13, § 3, first paragraph, of the Electricity Act stipulates that the CREG can make 
recommendations to the minister, after consulting the TSO, to amend the federal development plan 
during its term, if the planned investments are insufficient. Based on these recommendations, the 
minister can oblige the TSO to amend the federal development plan. However, the CREG's opinions 
are not binding. 
Therefore, the CREG cannot amend the federal development plan on its own initiative nor request the 
minister to amend it.  
Once the federal development plan is approved, the CREG's tariff methodology must enable 
implementation of the plan by the system operator (Art. 12, § 5, 4° of the Electricity Act). In other 
words, the CREG's competence is limited to verifying whether or not the costs of the investments 
included in the development plan are reasonable, according to the criteria set out in its tariff 
methodology. Investment costs deemed reasonable are included in the tariffs of the transmission 
system operator.

17 Wake losses are losses that reduce electricity production, whereby a distinction is made between internal and external wake 
effects. Internal wake effects refer to the interaction between wind turbines within the same offshore farm. A wind turbine 
extracts energy from the wind and uses it to produce electricity. This means that the energy in the wind decreases and the 
turbines behind the first turbine in the row consequently produce less energy. The higher the density of wind turbines within 
an offshore farm, the greater these internal wake effects will be and the lower the electricity production. External wake 
effects refer to the interaction between wind farms. If wind farms are close to each other, the wind cannot recover between 
them. In other words, the wake effect will then be greater and the electricity output will therefore be lower.

The transmission tariffs, as defined in the tariff methodology established by the CREG33, therefore 
allow to cover, in particular, depreciation and financial costs, including the remuneration of the system 
operator, which includes a fair margin for the remuneration of the capital invested in the grid.  
This fair margin is calculated each year by multiplying a percentage of the return by the value of the 
regulated asset base (RAB). This RAB is increased each year depending on the investment expenditure 
in the network, in particular that related to the MOG II. 
Therefore, the increase in investment expenditure for the MOG II has a direct impact on the evolution 
of financing and depreciation costs related to this infrastructure and, by extension, on the level of 
transmission tariffs that will be charged to end consumers over the next six decades. Indeed, the tariff 
methodology stipulates that the artificial island for the MOG II project should be depreciated over 60 
years. 
Assuming that interest rates in the financial markets remain stable, financing and depreciation costs 
will gradually increase in line with the investments made, until the full infrastructure is commissioned. 
Once commissioned, these costs will gradually decrease over the next six decades according to the 
recorded depreciation. Since the electricity infrastructure of the MOG II will be depreciated over a 
maximum period of 33 years and only the artificial island will be depreciated over a period of 60 years, 
these costs will fall faster in the first three decades than in the subsequent three decades. 
Under the assumption that the investment in the MOG II remains limited to € 7 billion - which is a low 
estimate if the DC part is retained - and that the full infrastructure is commissioned in 2034, the 
financing and depreciation costs related to the MOG II to be covered by the transmission tariffs would 
amount to around €629.5 million in 2034. Calculations made separately by both Elia and the CREG 
show that, without changes to the rules for allocating these costs, they would correspond to:

€7.7/MWh (about €27/year) for a residential consumer, i.e. an increase in transmission 
tariffs of approximately 67 % compared to the transmission tariffs applicable in 2024;

5.1. 
MOG II DESCRIPTION 
71. 
A detailed description of the MOG II project can be found in section 4.2.1 of the 2024-2034 
Federal Development Plan.40  
The MOG II has two objectives. First, it aims to integrate wind energy production from the Princess 
Elisabeth Zone into the Belgian and European electricity grid. Developing this zone will allow to install 
between 3.15 and 3.5 GW of wind capacity. Construction of the wind farms is planned in three parcels, 
with commissioning initially scheduled between 2028 and 203041. The wind turbines will be directly 
connected to the energy island via medium-voltage cables (66 kV).  Elia will then be responsible for 
converting this to high-voltage (220 kV) and exporting the electricity to the coast.  
The second objective of the MOG II is access to renewable energy produced elsewhere in the North 
Sea, thanks to interconnectors. The interconnectors provided for in the development plan are the 
Nautilus project, which connect Belgium to the UK, and the TritonLink project, which connects Belgium 
to Denmark. 
To ensure these functions, the approved MOG II design consists of the construction of an artificial 
island that will house the offshore electricity transmission infrastructure. The island will be connected 
to the coast by high-voltage alternating current (HVAC) and direct current (HVDC) cables. The diagram 
in Figure 3 illustrates this concept.

5.5. 
CONCLUSION 
137. The budget increases of the MOG II system can be attributed to: 
- 
the market developments for HVDC and AC infrastructure over the period 2022-2024, due 
to global shortage in the supply chain, and in particular for offshore applications. These 
market developments resulted in limited supply, with higher bids, less flexibility and 
increased risk for Elia; 
- 
Elia's focus on ensuring a grid design with high availability and operational flexibility led 
to assumptions and design choices that increased the cost base compared to typical 
offshore transmission designs. Examples of this include operation with a single AC node, 
the HVDC connection’s higher capacity and the inclusion of redundant and spare 
equipment; 
- 
the urge to stick to a strict timing for delivery as a result of which unverified assumptions  
led to premature design choices. The main factors in this regard are the following: the 
assumption that the offshore HVDC and AC substations could be built on the island via 
onshore techniques (stick-built method) and the technical and regulatory feasibility of 
using a single AC node. 
138. The external audit conducted by DNV shows that market developments did have a significant 
impact on the evolution of the costs of the MOG II, but that a number of elements linked to choices 
made by Elia also played a role in the significant cost increases.

139. This chapter evaluates the costs and benefits of the current MOG II design and other possible 
variants presented by Elia. 
140. The following cost-benefit analyses (hereafter, “CBA analyses”) aim to assess the social, 
economic and environmental impacts of an infrastructure project of general interest. The analyses 
therefore make it possible to compare the costs of this infrastructure project with its expected 
benefits, assess the social merits, inform the decision-making authorities and support political 
decision-making. When several designs are possible for the same infrastructure project, a cost-benefit 
analysis makes it possible to compare these alternatives and decide which alternative offers the most 
benefits in relation to the costs.  
141. The cost-benefit analyses therefore support the investment decision. 
6.1. 
GENERAL CONSIDERATIONS 
142. In the first instance, it should be recalled that the development and implementation of grid 
infrastructure projects is the transmission system operator’s core activity, in accordance with the 
applicable legal framework and taking into account the planned budgets and timetables. Cost-benefit 
analyses should generally be carried out prior to any decision to implement the project. Since Elia has 
the most complete and up-to-date information on the costs and benefits of an infrastructure project, 
it is the most appropriate party to carry out these cost-benefit analyses. Moreover, Elia has knowledge 
of both the Belgian and international electricity systems, the experience and the model to perform the 
necessary simulations to estimate the impact of a project on market prices in different countries and 
on the contribution to socio-economic welfare and the impact on society as a whole. 
143. The CREG on the other hand does not have the resources (either in terms of data or simulation 
models and the associated financial resources, human resources and experience) to reproduce the 
simulations made by Elia, and therefore it cannot validate their results. Based on a comparison of Elia's 
simulations - in which Elia was asked additional specific information questions - the CREG aimed to 
challenge and assess the consistency between the simulations and the plausibility of the results.  
144. Cost-benefit analyses - in the context of infrastructure works of general interest - should also be 
considered from the perspective of society. From Elia's perspective, all investments in the transmission 
network are remunerated with a profit margin via tariffs to the extent that the costs are not deemed 
unreasonable: the regulated asset base (RAB) increases with every investment. The business case for 
additional investments is therefore always positive from the perspective of the regulated transmission 
system operator, and independent from the added social value of these investments. 
145. In the context of the federal development plan, Elia performs cost-benefit analyses for the 
proposed, undecided projects for which the contribution to social economic welfare (SEW) can be 
calculated, namely for projects in the "horizontal system": interconnectors and the 380 kV grid.

148. To calculate the SEW, Elia uses the "Antares" model. First of all, it should be noted that any

simulating certain scenarios. Even if the model can give a close approximation of reality, the results of 
the simulations (outputs) of future scenarios depend on the data and hypotheses used (inputs).  
Since these are future scenarios, the underlying hypotheses are by definition uncertain (moreover, this 
uncertainty rises as the relevant time horizon broadens), but their impact on the results can be 
decisive. Consequently, the results of the simulations should be interpreted in the light of and within 
the limits of the hypotheses used.

Remarks by the CREG 
182. It should again be emphasised that the presented NPV values refer to the benefits for Belgian 
consumers, taking into account the impact on wholesale electricity prices and congestion rents. The 
impact of variation in wholesale electricity prices on the producer surplus is not considered here.  
183. The calculation in Excel of the NPV values was submitted by Elia to the CREG on 8 January 2025. 
The CREG believes that this calculation provides a good basis for comparison, but nevertheless has 
some reservations. 
184. A first important observation concerns the impact of electricity demand. As can also be seen 
from Table 14, the NPV increases as electricity consumption increases. This is explained by the fact 
that the NPV is the present value of the product of the net consumer impact (in €/MWh) in each year 
and electricity consumption in the same year. An estimate of high future electricity consumption leads 
to higher benefits on the one hand, which translates into a higher NPV, and to a lower cost per MWh 
on the other (given that the cost is covered by more MWh).  
However, if the evolution of electricity consumption is lower than its current estimate, this results in a 
double negative effect: on the one hand, the benefits decrease (as far as they were positive), but on 
the other hand, the cost per MWh increases (the same investment cost will have to be covered by 
fewer MWh). 
To reduce the risk of this double negative effect, it is recommended as a precaution to estimate the 
evolution of electricity consumption with moderation (and best not to overestimate it). The graph 
below shows the evolution of electricity consumption used by Elia in its calculations, with the addition 
of the recent historical values for 2023 and 2024.

be connected. Support for wind farms in the Princess Elisabeth Zone is therefore inextricably linked to 
the construction of the necessary grid infrastructure. Conversely, building the grid infrastructure on 
the island is inextricably linked to the objective of connecting and linking these wind farms to the 
Belgian mainland.  Given that the future domain concessionaire will not necessarily be a Belgian 
company, possibly not even a European one, the effect of subsidies is not neutral from a Belgian 
perspective. The benefits derived from offshore wind energy production in the Princess Elisabeth Zone, 
including the impact on the electricity price due to wind energy produced in the PEZ, would not exist 
without the support of the wind farms. For these reasons, the CREG believes it is useful, in the context

196. In the current context of the energy transition, a European comparison of the evolution of 
investment costs in electricity grid infrastructure provides an additional tool to objectify the cost 
evolution, especially with regard to the development of offshore grid assets.  
197. A comparative evaluation makes it possible to highlight both the factors that different countries 
have in common, and their specific national characteristics, and draw relevant lessons.  
Identifying best practices can also provide inspiration to help optimise investment expenditure and 
promote the efficient use of resources, in line with the CREG's recommendations in its white paper 'No 
transition without transmission - Financing strategic network investments as part of the energy 
transition'97.  
Finally, comparing practices and costs at the European level provides a better understanding of the 
transparency and justification of planned investments at national level. These are essential criteria for 
gaining the trust of consumers and/or taxpayers, who basically have to bear the costs.  
These requirements regarding transparency and the social responsibility of investments are all the 
more acute as the development of offshore transmission infrastructure will lead to a significant and 
accelerated increase in the Regulated Asset Base (RAB) of the transmission system operator, who 
remains the owner of these transmission assets. 
198. From this perspective, the CREG, as the national regulator responsible for tariff aspects relating 
to grid infrastructure, has proposed setting up a specific European working group within CEER, bringing 
together 30 member countries98. 
199. In the shorter term, and in order to contribute as much as possible to the debate and immediate 
challenges at the Belgian level, the CREG recently surveyed its European counterparts, to have first 
indicative insights at the European level. To this end, it organised several bilateral meetings and sent 
out a questionnaire to European regulators adapted to the urgent circumstances of the exercise 
conducted in December 2024.

204. Based on the obtained information, this increase can be attributed to several factors, the relative 
importance of which varies. Reasons or drivers indicated by respondents include:  
- 
inflation (indexation of contracts); 
- 
the increase in raw material costs; 
- 
the increase in labour costs (construction work); 
- 
tensions in the supply chain (in particular due to the limited number of suppliers and the 
increasing difficulty in finding suppliers who can deliver for specific deadlines);  
- 
the rise in interest rates in the capital market;  
- 
exchange rate fluctuations;  
- 
energy price fluctuations. 
205. While tensions in the supply chain, increased commodity prices and inflation are the main 
drivers of the overall increase in investment costs, the fact remains that in some cases (e.g. Ireland and 
the Netherlands) this increase is also amplified by initial underestimates of the investment costs 
(CAPEX and/or OPEX). 
206. Indeed, there is a growing general awareness of cost increases and their potential impact on grid 
tariffs at the European level.  
207. To minimise the impact of this increase, or even the escalation of investment costs (Ireland, the 
Netherlands), risk mitigation measures have been considered, discussed or undertaken in some 
countries by the relevant public authorities. These include the following: 
- 
updating the underlying analyses of investment projects to verify their effectiveness in a 
context of higher costs;  
- 
a reassessment of the investment needs (Italy and Spain); 
- 
prioritisation of investments (Italy); 
- 
a postponement or even cancellation of investment projects;  
For instance, while the project to build the Bornholm energy island in Denmark is still 
ongoing, the North Sea Energy Island project has been put on hold indefinitely. It is even 
possible that this project will never happen. Market tensions may increase or decrease by 
then, but the final decision lies with the decision-making authorities. 
In Italy, specific attention is given to postponing or even cancelling less favourable 
investment projects, although these projects may have potential benefits that outweigh 
the costs.

208. Besides these risk mitigation measures, we should also mention the recommendation of the 
Dutch regulator ACM. The aim is to ensure that the government, and more generally the public, 
receives as much relevant and reliable information as possible from the system operator regarding 
cost estimates, risks, interdependencies and possible consequences of large-scale infrastructure 
investments. There is currently no guarantee that the system operator will do this and share all 
relevant and reliable information at its disposal. However, this information is crucial to ensure that 
informed decisions are taken regarding the future of the country's energy system. Constructive 
discussions are currently ongoing with the minister and the grid operator to strengthen the legal 
framework - in particular the additional powers and roles the regulator should have in this context - in 
order to improve the overall framework for controlling and monitoring investment projects in 
electricity grid infrastructure.  
209. The Irish regulator, CER, fully agrees with this and urges that:  
- 
data on the economic analysis underpinning major projects and policy choices are 
transparently communicated between government departments involved in the energy 
transition;  
- 
after the initial analysis, the relevant data related to a cost increase are shared 
transparently and regularly, so that there is continuous awareness of the cost increase 
(when this occurs) and the possible consequences. 
Finally, given the fact that Europe is highly dependent on a limited number of foreign suppliers, namely

respect, further discussions about supply chain tensions in the context of the debate on European 
competitiveness, as well as a follow-up on future challenges (identifying needs and prioritising 
projects) would help provide relevant insights.

8. 
RECOMMENDATIONS OF THE CREG REGARDING THE 
LEGAL FRAMEWORK 
210. Given the impact of the federal development plan on grid tariffs, it is crucial to enhance the 
transparency and reliability of the approval process to ensure that it is based on solid and verifiable 
foundations. In this context, the obligations of the transmission system operator (TSO) should be 
strengthened, so that they present a figures-based plan with a detailed cost-benefit analyses for each 
major investment project (including the different options and variants). The CREG also recommends 
introducing an alert mechanism, by requiring that the TSO provides a detailed report in the event of a 
significant increase in investment costs for a major project, or a significant overrun of the planned 
implementation deadlines, so that the CREG can propose changes to the development plan where 
appropriate. 
The CREG also asks tobe explicitly involved at each stage of the drafting of the development plan, to 
help define the plan's objectives and priorities from the outset. To this end, the CREG should be able 
to verify the identification of needs and the cost-benefit analyses, with guaranteed access to all data 
deemed relevant. This enhanced role will allow the CREG to ensure the adequacy and transparency of 
investments in the grid more effectively, to meet the needs of the energy transition while protecting 
the interests of consumers. 
The CREG asks that its collaboration with the General Directorate of Energy and the Federal Planning 
Bureau be formalised. A collaboration and monitoring committee made up of these three bodies 
should be set up for the development plan. In order to have a mechanism to exert influence prior to 
the adoption of the federal development plan, the CREG also requests that it is able to issue a unified, 
i.e. binding, opinion on the development plan and that the deadline for issuing this opinion be 
extended to at least 60 days, instead of the current 40 days, to allow it to make more in-depth analyses. 
Furthermore, the CREG recommends including in Article 12, § 5 of the Electricity Act a guideline on 
tariffs that encourages the system operator to improve its budget estimates for major investment 
projects and foster proactive management to avoid budget overruns that are inadequately justified by 
exceptional circumstances. Such overruns may have consequences on the recovery of their costs 
and/or the corresponding compensation through tariffs. 
The impact of the cost increase due to the MOG II will primarily be felt in the subsequent tariff periods. 
When the new tariff methodology for the 2028-2032 period will be drafted, the CREG will examine 
possible alternatives to the existing regulatory model - where the remuneration for the system 
operator is largely determined by the scale of the investments - to encourage the latter to optimise 
the use of existing assets and minimise investment costs.

9. 
CONCLUSION 
9.1. 
GENERAL CONCLUSIONS 
In view of the foregoing analyses, the following can be stated: 
- 
the CREG, despite its multidisciplinary expertise, currently has too limited legal powers in 
the context of the drafting, approval and possible revision of network development plans 
and in the context of monitoring infrastructure projects developed by the TSO; 
- 
the CREG faced numerous difficulties in the context of monitoring cost evolution in 
connection with the MOG II dossier. These include in particular:  
• 
the TSO's lack of transparency regarding all the information which is useful for the 
proper exercise of this oversight;  
• 
the lack of quantitative and qualitative analyses that were essential before the 
federal government approved the design of the MOG II in 2021; 
• 
the TSO's refusal to analyse alternative options for the design of the MOG II at the 
request of the CREG in June 2024, to which there was no positive follow-up until 
December 2024, in order to weigh up the alternatives and make informed 
investment choices in the public interest and in the interest of Belgian consumers; 
• 
Up until October 2024, the lack of a proactive and timely communication by the 
TSO to the competent authorities regarding important developments in the case, 
including the identified budget overrun and the unilateral decisions of the TSO 
relating in particular to significant changes in the choice of technical options that 
led to the budget increases.  
- 
The budget for the MOG II was revised upwards, from € 2.2 billion in 2021 to 
[CONFIDENTIAL: between € 7 and 8 billion] currently, according to Elia's latest estimates. 
If the current design and budget of the MOG II remain unchanged, the cost, to be covered 
by transmission tariffs, is expected to be around € 800 million per year from 2034, when 
the full infrastructure will be commissioned. 
These costs correspond to € 9.8/MWh (about € 34.4/year) for a residential consumer and 
€ 4.45/MWh for a large industrial consumer directly connected to the Elia 
380/220/150 kV transmission grid.

At the European level, a general rising trend in investment costs can also be seen in the 
context of large infrastructure projects, including offshore grid assets. While tensions in 
the supply chain, higher commodity prices and inflation are the main drivers for this 
general rise in investment costs, the fact remains that such rises can also be exacerbated

minimise the impact of this rise, or even escalation, of the investment costs, risk 
mitigation measures are also being considered and discussed by the different countries

9.2. 
RECOMMENDATIONS 
The recommendations below aim to improve the regulatory framework for investment projects in grid 
infrastructure. They pertain both to the MOG II project examined here and to investment projects that 
are currently ongoing or future projects. 
First, it appears crucial that grid infrastructure projects, as they address issues of public interest, be 
subject to legal procedures that guarantee, in particular, the objectivity and transparency of all 
qualitative and quantitative analyses underpinning them. This requirement is essential to ensure that 
the results of these analyses only serve the public interest, without being influenced by potential 
conflicts of interest or other specific considerations.  
To ensure that the public interest remains the absolute priority in guiding the decisive investment 
choices for the energy future of Belgium, the Electricity Act should be amended with regard to:  
- 
the current procedure for preparing, approving and following-up grid development plans, 
in particular by:  
• 
strengthening the role of the public authorities, including that of the CREG; 
• 
obliging the system operator to present, at the very early stages of the 
development plan, all possible investment options, backed up by quantified 
analyses to meet the same grid need, so that the authorities have a certain decision-
making flexibility, to then adequately describe the conceptual design in the 
development plan, to avoid situations where the competent and decision-making 
authorities are in a bind when alternative choices and courses of action are 
restricted or even non-existent, to the detriment of the public interest; 
• 
a strict mechanism for budgetary oversight of the investment projects of the 
transmission system operator, including periodic reports to the competent and 
decision-making authorities, describing the evolution of costs compared to initial 
estimates, as well as the explanatory factors for any identified discrepancies, so 
that any budget overruns or even escalations are identified, and corrective 
measures can be assessed in good time. 
- 
the tariff framework to ensure better control of investment costs and foster proactive 
management on the part of the transmission system operator in the interest of grid users 
through a new provision in the tariff guidelines to provide tangible impetus for strict 
budgetary oversight;
